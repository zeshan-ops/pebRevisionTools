## Two hurdles, not one

Design right subsists in an **original** design (s.213(1)). "Original" carries
**two** requirements, and candidates routinely give only the first:

1. **Original in the copyright sense** — the design must originate with the
   author and not be copied.
2. **Not commonplace** — s.213(4): a design "is not 'original' for the purposes
   of this Part if it is commonplace in a qualifying country in the design field
   in question at the time of its creation."

A design can be entirely the designer's own work and still fail, because it is
commonplace. Say both.

## Unpacking s.213(4)

Four elements, each of which can be argued on the facts:

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Commonplace** | Trite, common or run-of-the-mill in the field — not merely known to exist |
| **In a qualifying country** | Assessed across qualifying countries, not the UK alone (s.217(3)) |
| **In the design field in question** | The relevant field is the one the article belongs to, which needs identifying on the facts |
| **At the time of its creation** | Fixed at creation, unlike registered design novelty, which runs to the filing or priority date |

## Contrast with registered designs

A frequent source of confusion, and worth stating explicitly in an answer:

| | UK unregistered design right | UK registered design |
|---|---|---|
| Test | Original + not commonplace | New + individual character |
| Assessed at | Time of **creation** | **Filing or priority** date |
| Compared against | The design field in qualifying countries | Any design made available to the public |
| Copying | Must be **proved** — independent creation is a defence | Irrelevant; a monopoly right |

The copying point is the one the 2022 examiner's report singles out: candidates
were reminded that proof of copying is an essential requirement of infringement
for unregistered design protection.
